Mean, variance & standard deviation

Five students take a test. Can we capture the whole class in just a number or two? Follow that one question and you’ll build the three most important summaries in statistics — no background needed.

Step 1 · where is the center?

Meet the data, and find its middle

Here are five scores on a number line. Each dot is one student. Drag them around if you like — this same little dataset follows us through the whole page.

First question: what’s a single “typical” score?

The natural answer is the mean (the average): add every value, divide by how many there are. Picture it as a see-saw — the mean is the balance point, the spot where the dots on the left pull exactly as hard as the dots on the right.

Step 2 · one number hides too much

The mean can’t tell these classes apart

Here’s the catch. Both classes below have the exact same mean of 85 — yet they’re nothing alike. Class A is bunched tightly together; Class B is split to the extremes. The mean alone is blind to the difference.

Second question: how spread out are the values?

We need a second number that measures spread. That number is the variance — and building it up is the heart of this page.

Step 3 · measuring the spread

Variance, built one piece at a time

How do we turn “spread” into a number? Start with the obvious idea: how far is each point from the mean? Then handle a snag, and you’ve got the variance. Click through the four steps and watch each part of the formula light up the picture.

The variance.  —  “the average of the squared distances from the mean.”
Why square the distances? Step 1 shows the snag: the raw distances, with their + and − signs, always cancel to zero. Squaring fixes that (everything turns positive) and it makes far-away points count for much more — twice as far becomes four times the area.
Step 4 · a wrinkle worth understanding

Wait — why divide by n − 1?

You may have noticed step 4 divided by n − 1, not n. That looks like a typo, but it’s deliberate, and here’s the reason you can actually watch. We’ll sample over and over from a population whose true variance we know to be 1, and compare the two recipes.

population: sample size n = 5
true σ² = 1.00
÷ (n−1) average → on σ²
÷ n average → too low
where ÷ n lands: σ²·(n−1)/n
Dividing by n is biased low — on average it lands at , short of the truth. Dividing by n − 1 corrects it exactly. Why? The distances are measured from the sample’s own mean, which hugs its points more tightly than the true mean does — so the squares come out a touch too small, and n − 1 makes up the difference. (Equivalently: fix the mean and all but one point, and the last is forced — only n − 1 are free to move.) The gap is biggest for tiny samples and fades as n grows.
Step 5 · back to a number you can read

Standard deviation: spread in plain units

Variance has one awkward quirk. Because we squared everything, its units are squared too — if scores are in points, variance is in points². What does a “squared point” even mean? Nothing you can feel. So we undo the squaring with a square root, and land on the standard deviation.

The standard deviation.  —  the typical distance of a point from the mean, back in the data’s own units.
A subtle point: standard deviation is not the plain average of the distances. The squaring lets far points pull harder, so SD comes out a bit larger than the average gap. (That plain average has its own name — the “mean absolute deviation” — and it’s a different number.)

one last distinction

Your sample vs the whole population

Everything above used the data in front of you — a sample. Usually that sample is a stand-in for a much bigger population you can’t fully measure. The ideas are identical; only the notation and that divisor change. Your sample numbers are your best estimate of the population’s.
