measure 1 · center

Mean — the balance point

The mean adds up every value and divides by how many there are. Geometrically, it’s the point where the data balances: the pull from the low side equals the pull from the high side.

measure 2 · spread

Variance — built one piece at a time

Variance measures spread as the average squared distance from the mean. The formula looks dense, so let’s light up one piece at a time and watch what each does to the picture.

Definition. Sample variance  ·  population variance .
Why square the distances? Two reasons you can see above: signed deviations cancel to zero (so raw distances can’t measure spread), and squaring punishes far points — twice as far makes a square four times bigger.
measure 2 · the fine print

Why divide by n − 1, not n?

Step 4 divided by n − 1, not n. Here’s the reason, made visible. We sample many times from a population whose true variance we know, and compare the two recipes.

population: sample size n = 5
true σ² = 1.00
÷ (n−1) average → lands on σ²
÷ n average → falls short
where ÷ n lands: σ²·(n−1)/n
The ÷ n recipe is biased low — it averages out to , below the truth. Dividing by n − 1 corrects it exactly (unbiased). The gap is — worst for small samples, vanishing as n grows.

The intuition: the deviations are measured from the sample’s own mean, which sits as close to its points as mathematically possible — closer than the true population mean does. So the squared distances come out a touch too small. There’s also a degrees-of-freedom view: once you know the mean and any n − 1 points, the last point is forced (the deviations must sum to zero) — so only n − 1 of them are free.

measure 3 · spread, readable

Standard deviation — back to a length

Variance is an area in squared units. Take its square root and you get a length in the data’s own units: the typical distance of a point from the mean.

Definition.  ·  population . Same units as the data.
Standard deviation is not the plain average of the distances. Because of the squaring, far points count more — so SD is a bit larger than the average gap (that plain average is a different quantity, the “mean absolute deviation”).
