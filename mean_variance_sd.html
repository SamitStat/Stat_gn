Act 1 · the mean

A balance point, not a “typical” value

Slide the triangle’s data around and the teal fulcrum always settles where the pull from the left exactly equals the pull from the right. That’s the mean: the one spot where the deviations sum to zero, Σ(xᵢ − x̄) = 0 — the data’s centre of gravity.

Try the 70 / 100 class preset. The mean is 85 — yet nobody scored 85. The mean is a balance point, not “what a typical student got,” and it need not be a value anyone actually has.
Act 2 · variance

Why we square the distances

Turn on deviations: each point gets a signed distance to the mean. Add them up and you always get zero — the positives and negatives cancel (that’s the balance again). So raw distances can’t measure spread.

Turn on squares: each deviation grows into a literal square whose area is the squared distance. Squaring kills the signs and punishes far points hard — drag a point twice as far and its square gets four times bigger. The variance is the average of these square areas.

Variance lives in squared units (points², cm²…). That’s why it’s hard to feel directly — it’s an area, not a distance.
Act 3 · standard deviation

Bringing it back to a length

Turn on the ±SD band. Standard deviation is just √variance — the side of the “average square” laid back down on the number line. Now it’s in the data’s own units: a typical distance from the mean. Small SD = tight cluster; large SD = wide spread.

Standard deviation is not the average of the raw distances. Because of the squaring, far points count for more — so SD is a little larger than the plain average gap. (Statisticians call the plain average gap the “mean absolute deviation,” a different number.)
Act 4 · the deep reason

Why the mean, of all points?

Here’s the property that makes the mean special. Drag the dashed guess line to any centre you like; from every point we draw a square to your guess and add up the areas — the total squared distance. Watch the bowl below: the total is smallest at exactly one place.

The minimum sits exactly at the mean. Of every possible centre, the mean is the one that makes the total squared distance as small as possible — it is the least-squares centre. (Curious aside: if you added up un-squared distances instead, the winner would be the median. Squaring is what crowns the mean.) This single idea is the seed of linear regression.

The fine print

Why divide by n − 1 and not n? (Bessel’s correction)
When you only have a sample, you measure spread around the sample’s own mean — and by Act 4, the points are as close to their own mean as mathematically possible (it’s the least-squares centre). So the squared distances come out a touch too small compared with spread around the true population mean you actually care about. Dividing by n − 1 instead of n nudges the estimate back up to compensate.

The “degrees of freedom” view: once you know the mean and any n − 1 of the points, the last point is forced (the deviations must sum to zero). Only n − 1 distances are truly free — so we average over n − 1. Flip the toggle above and watch the variance jump. For a whole population, you divide by n.
Sample vs population: x̄ & s² vs μ & σ²
Same idea, two contexts. Sample = the data in front of you: mean x̄ = (1/n) Σ xᵢ, variance s² = (1/(n−1)) Σ(xᵢ−x̄)². Population = the whole world you’re really after: mean μ = (1/N) Σ xᵢ, variance σ² = (1/N) Σ(xᵢ−μ)² = E[(X−μ)²]. And the expected value E[X] = μ. The sample numbers are your best estimates of the population ones.
The worked example, step by step
Data 2, 4.5, 6, 7.5, 10 → mean 6.0. Deviations −4, −1.5, 0, +1.5, +4; squared 16, 2.25, 0, 2.25, 16; sum 36.5. Sample variance = 36.5 / (5−1) = 9.125, so SD = √9.125 ≈ 3.02. (Load the 5 points preset to see exactly this on the stage.)
