Where E[X] comes from — the probability model
The cast

One number here — three different questions

In these demos μ, E[X], and x̄ land on the same value. The point is not that they're the same — it's that they answer three different questions and happen to agree when the world behaves.

μPopulation meanREAL · HIDDEN
“What is actually true?”
The average of every member of the real population. A fixed constant that exists even though you usually can't measure it — you'd have to survey everyone.
μ = (1/N) · Σ X
E[X]Expected valueMODEL · CALCULATED
“What does the model say?”
The probability-weighted average of a random variable — a property of the distribution you chose. You compute it; you never roll it.
E[X] = Σ xᵢ·P(xᵢ)  =  ∫ x·f(x) dx
x̄Sample meanDATA · MEASURED
“What did I observe?”
The average of the values actually in your sample. The only one you can always compute from data — but it changes every time you draw a new sample.
x̄ = (1/n) · Σ xᵢ

The bridge
The sample mean x̄ is an estimate of the expected value E[X]. By the Law of Large Numbers, as n → ∞ the estimate converges to the target — the orange line settling onto the blue line above. And if your probability model truly describes the population, then E[X] = μ. So the three meet by two different routes: one by a limit (data → model), one by an assumption (model → world). They're equal here because the world is behaving — not by definition.
How to read it
🎯
Blue line = E[X]
The fixed target from the model. It never moves while you sample — for the die it sits at 3.5, a value you can't even roll.
📈
Orange line = x̄ₙ
The mean recomputed after every draw. Wild early, then it calms — and it can drift away before coming back. Convergence isn't a straight line.
🟦
Blue band = ±1.96σ/√n
The range the mean should sit in 95% of the time. It pinches inward as n grows — the estimate sharpens.
👻
Faint lines = past runs
Each “New run” leaves a ghost. Different paths, same destination — a small sample is luck, a large one is law.
📊
Lower chart
The distribution filling in. Bars (your data) rise to meet the dots (true probabilities) — the same convergence, one level down.
Try this

Run it, then hit “New run” five times. At a glance the early paths scatter all over; every one still ends up hugging the blue line. That fan of ghosts is sampling variability — and it shrinks as n grows.

Watch the two numbers under the chart move opposite ways. The relative gap shrinks toward 0% while the absolute surplus keeps growing. The average gets closer even though the raw imbalance gets bigger — proof that “the law of averages” evening-out is a myth. A die has no memory; a 6 is never “due.”

Switch die → grades → normal. The blue target jumps to 3.5, then 76, then 3, and the mean re-chases it each time. E[X] is a property of the model you picked — change the model, move the target. Note no student scores exactly 76, yet 76 is the expected grade.

Use “jump to n” to teleport to 500,000. The line is pinned to the target and the band is a sliver — but x̄ still isn't exactly E[X]. It estimates the target; it never becomes it. With infinite data the gap would vanish.

Notation
Population mean: μ = (1/N) Σ X
Expected value (discrete): E[X] = Σ xᵢ P(xᵢ)
Expected value (continuous): E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx
Sample mean: x̄ = (1/n) Σ xᵢ
Law of Large Numbers: x̄ → E[X] as n → ∞
If model = population: E[X] = μ
